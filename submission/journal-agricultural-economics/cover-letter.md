Please consider our manuscript, **“Does Land Rental Prevalence Signal Market Effectiveness? Cross-Country Evidence on Agricultural TFP Growth,”** for publication in the *Journal of Agricultural Economics*.

The manuscript addresses a gap between the large microeconomic literature on land-rental markets and the use of aggregate land-market indicators in international agricultural analysis. Rather than estimating another country-specific effect of rental participation, we ask whether harmonised national measures of rental prevalence contain information about subsequent agricultural total factor productivity growth.

We link FAO agricultural-census indicators for 65 countries with USDA Economic Research Service agricultural TFP data and World Bank development indicators. A 10-percentage-point higher rental-area share is associated with a -0.0011 change in annualised five-year post-census TFP growth (HC3 p = 0.598), and this interpretation remains stable across predefined robustness checks. The design is explicitly associational and all quantitative results are generated through a reproducible R-based workflow.

The contribution is not another estimate of whether land rental can improve productivity within a particular country. Instead, the paper provides a cross-country boundary test of whether the mechanism documented in the microeconomic literature survives aggregation into a readily observable national indicator. To our knowledge, the existing literature has not established whether harmonised cross-country census measures of rental prevalence provide a systematic signal of subsequent national agricultural productivity dynamics. Our results suggest that rental-market prevalence should not be treated as an interchangeable proxy for rental-market effectiveness.

We believe this distinction is relevant to the readership of the *Journal of Agricultural Economics* because it bears directly on the interpretation of internationally comparable land-market indicators, agricultural resource allocation and the design of evidence for land-market policy. Transaction prevalence is substantially easier to observe across countries than the institutional frictions that determine the quality and direction of land reallocation; our findings show the limits of using the former as a proxy for the latter.

The manuscript is original, is not under consideration elsewhere, and all authors have reviewed and approved the submitted version. Source data are publicly available from FAO, USDA ERS and the World Bank. All quantitative results are generated from documented source data and R code, and reproducibility materials can be provided in accordance with the journal's requirements.

OpenAI ChatGPT was used to support literature-search planning, manuscript organisation, language editing and reproducibility-workflow planning and review.
